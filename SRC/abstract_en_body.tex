Medical image analysis extracts information about organs, lesions, and tissue structures from medical images, thereby supporting disease diagnosis and treatment planning. Existing deep learning methods commonly assume that training data can be centrally collected, annotations are relatively complete, and data distributions remain stable. However, these assumptions often fail in real clinical environments. Medical centers, imaging devices, and patient populations change over time, while new disease categories, anatomical structures, and imaging modalities enter clinical workflows. Meanwhile, privacy protection, storage constraints, and annotation costs make it difficult to centralize raw data from multiple centers, retain and repeatedly access historical samples, or obtain more than sparse annotations for the current task. The combined effects of continual task evolution and limited training information require medical image models to acquire new knowledge while preserving previously learned capabilities.

To address these challenges, this thesis investigates three aspects of continual medical image analysis: scenario construction and evaluation for continual medical image segmentation, replay-free federated continual medical image classification, and weakly supervised continual segmentation and continual medical image registration. The main contributions are summarized as follows.

(1) To address the lack of unified task scenarios, dataset construction, and evaluation criteria in continual medical image segmentation, as well as the inability of a single average-performance or forgetting metric to fully characterize model capabilities, this thesis proposes a comprehensive evaluation framework for continual medical image segmentation. Based on changes in the input distribution, label space, and segmentation target, the framework defines three scenarios: Domain-CL, Class-CL, and Organ-CL. It standardizes task sequences, model outputs, training budgets, and test settings, and evaluates representative methods in terms of final performance, historical knowledge retention, current-task learning, forward generalization, and resource costs. Experimental results show that lower forgetting does not necessarily lead to better new-task learning or forward generalization, and that different types of methods exhibit clear trade-offs among stability, plasticity, and resource costs. These findings demonstrate that a single metric is insufficient for a comprehensive assessment of continual segmentation models and that model capabilities should instead be evaluated from multiple dimensions.

(2) To address the difficulty of protecting historical knowledge across participating centers when multi-center raw images cannot be centralized and historical samples cannot be replayed, this thesis proposes a gradient-subspace aggregation method for replay-free federated continual learning. Each participating center recursively constructs a low-rank principal gradient subspace from sample-wise gradients of the current task, while the server aggregates historical protection directions from different centers. During subsequent local training, gradients are projected onto the orthogonal complement of the protected subspace, thereby reducing interference from new-task updates with historical knowledge accumulated across centers. On this basis, an adaptive aggregation strategy is further proposed to select related centers according to layer-wise subspace distances and construct a corresponding protection space for each center. Experiments on multiple medical image datasets show that the proposed method improves overall classification performance and historical knowledge retention without storing or replaying historical raw samples, while maintaining robust performance under different levels of data heterogeneity, numbers of centers, and participation rates. Further analysis shows that uniform aggregation is more effective in protecting historical knowledge shared across centers, whereas adaptive aggregation better accommodates new-task learning by reducing constraints from unrelated directions. Moreover, subspace information is transmitted only after each task, resulting in limited additional communication costs.

(3) To address insufficient supervision, restricted access to historical data, and catastrophic forgetting when medical image segmentation and registration tasks arrive sequentially, this thesis proposes a scribble-supervised continual segmentation method and a sharpness-aware meta-continual registration method. For segmentation, the proposed method enhances supervision through global consistency and spatial priors, while combining image–scribble replay with feature constraints to preserve the model’s segmentation capability on historical tasks. Experimental results show that supervision enhancement improves segmentation performance on the current task, while limited replay and feature constraints help alleviate historical knowledge forgetting. For continual registration, a limited number of historical image pairs are replayed to retain information from previous tasks, and a meta-learning mechanism is integrated with sharpness-aware training to coordinate new-task adaptation and historical capability retention. Experiments on four sequential registration tasks spanning different organs and imaging modalities show that the proposed method mitigates the degradation of early-task performance caused by direct sequential training and improves generalization to unseen tasks.

In summary, this thesis addresses the central challenge of balancing new-knowledge acquisition, historical capability retention, and cross-scenario generalization in medical image models under continual task evolution and limited training information. It proposes a scenario construction and multidimensional evaluation framework for continual medical image segmentation, a gradient-subspace aggregation method for federated continual learning, and continual learning methods for scribble-supervised segmentation and medical image registration. These studies provide theoretical and experimental support for continual model updating, capability evaluation, and method development in medical image analysis under limited training information.
