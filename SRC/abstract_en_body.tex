Medical image analysis supports disease screening, diagnosis, and treatment planning through segmentation, classification, and registration. As medical centers, disease categories, organs, and imaging modalities change, models need to learn new tasks while retaining existing capabilities. In practice, limited training information creates three challenges. Sparse annotations provide insufficient guidance for learning complete anatomical structures. Distributed data and restricted access to historical samples make knowledge preservation across clients difficult. Changes in organs and modalities alter registration objectives and deformation distributions, making adaptation and retention difficult to reconcile with only a few historical image pairs. These challenges constrain continual model updates and the retention of existing capabilities. This thesis studies continual medical image analysis under evolving tasks and limited training information by designing an evaluation platform and developing methods for weakly supervised segmentation, replay-free federated classification, and registration with bounded replay.

To address inconsistent task settings and incomplete capability assessment, this thesis designs the MedCL evaluation platform. MedCL distinguishes medical analysis tasks, incremental settings, and training conditions. It associates stage models or predictions with fixed test references, uses stage--task performance matrices to evaluate historical retention and current-task learning, and analyzes resource use from training records. A continual segmentation benchmark is then constructed to cover cross-center domain shifts, incremental cardiac structures, and cross-organ tasks. Representative methods are compared under shared data splits, network architectures, and training settings. The results show that reducing forgetting does not necessarily improve new-task learning or generalization to future domains; performance also depends on parameter growth, replay capacity, and task order. This evaluation design provides a common foundation for the subsequent method studies.

To address insufficient supervision and historical performance degradation in continual segmentation, this thesis proposes ZSDERpp, which combines supervision enhancement with feature consistency. Global consistency and spatial priors provide additional constraints for unlabeled regions. Bounded replay of historical image--scribble pairs and reference-feature preservation then support continued optimization of old-task samples while limiting representation drift. For incremental classes, background-semantic handling is incorporated to make old-class retention compatible with learning new structures. Experiments reuse the benchmark's patient splits, task orders, and dense test references in six-center domain-incremental and three-stage class-incremental settings. The experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in improving segmentation performance and historical retention under fixed simulated scribbles.

To address replay-free federated continual classification with heterogeneous client distributions, this thesis proposes FedSubMerge, which fuses principal gradient subspaces across clients. Each client constructs a low-rank representation of historical gradients, and the server aggregates the resulting protection directions. Subsequent updates to shared parameters are projected onto the orthogonal complement of the protection space to reduce interference with historical knowledge. An adaptive fusion strategy further selects related clients layer by layer, reducing restrictions imposed by unrelated historical directions. Experiments consider sequentially arriving class batches and task-specific classification heads with known test-time task identity. Evaluation covers distribution skew, label-quantity skew, and source-related feature skew. The results demonstrate the effectiveness of both fusion strategies without replaying historical raw samples, while their relative performance varies with client distributions.

To reconcile adaptation and retention as organs and modalities change, this thesis proposes SAMCL, a continual registration method combining bounded experience replay, first-order meta-continual learning, and sharpness-aware optimization. A small set of historical fixed--moving image pairs supplies old-task training signals. Meta-continual learning coordinates updates from current and historical samples, while sharpness-aware optimization accounts for loss variation in the parameter neighborhood. Following the platform's stage-based evaluation, structural overlap and landmark error are used separately to assess registration accuracy, retention, and current-task modeling ability. Experiments use a sequence of brain, abdominal, lung, and cross-modal abdominal registration tasks, with additional analyses of replay capacity and within-domain and cross-task generalization. The results show reduced historical degradation during sequential updates across tasks and improved structural overlap on multiple registration tasks.
