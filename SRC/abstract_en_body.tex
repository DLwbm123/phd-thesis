Medical image analysis provides quantitative support for disease diagnosis, treatment planning, and outcome assessment by modeling organs, lesions, and tissue morphology. Deep learning has achieved strong performance in medical image segmentation, classification, and registration. Most existing methods, however, assume that training data can be collected centrally, annotations are sufficiently complete, and data distributions remain largely stationary. These assumptions are often violated in clinical practice. Data from different medical centers and scanners arrive over time, while new disease categories, anatomical structures, and imaging modalities are continually introduced. At the same time, privacy requirements, data-governance rules, storage constraints, and annotation costs restrict the information available for model updates. Multi-center raw data usually cannot be centralized, historical samples may not be retained indefinitely, and current tasks may provide only sparse annotations. The combination of evolving tasks and limited training information makes a model prone to losing previously acquired capabilities when learning new knowledge and creates complex trade-offs among current-task learning, historical knowledge retention, and forward generalization.

To address these challenges, this thesis investigates continual learning for medical image analysis under evolving tasks and limited training information. The study covers scenario definition and evaluation for continual medical image segmentation, replay-free federated continual medical image classification, continual segmentation with scribble supervision, and continual medical image registration. The main contributions are summarized as follows.

(1) To resolve inconsistencies in scenario definitions, historical-information access, and evaluation protocols for continual medical image segmentation, this thesis establishes a systematic benchmark. According to changes in the input distribution, label space, and segmentation target, continual segmentation is organized into Domain-CL for sequential changes across institutions or acquisition domains, Class-CL for incremental expansion of the label space, and Organ-CL for changes across anatomical targets and imaging modalities. The benchmark standardizes task sequences, model outputs, training budgets, and test protocols. Beyond final average performance and forgetting, it evaluates current-task modeling, historical knowledge retention, forward generalization to future tasks, and resource cost. The experiments show that methods behave differently across these dimensions. Lower forgetting does not necessarily imply stronger current-task learning or forward generalization, and gains from replay or parameter expansion must be interpreted together with their resource costs. This study provides a common basis for fair comparison and for defining the scope of conclusions in continual medical image segmentation.

(2) For federated learning in which multi-center raw data cannot be centralized, historical raw samples cannot be replayed, and diagnostic tasks arrive sequentially, this thesis proposes FedSubMerge based on gradient-subspace fusion. Each client recursively constructs a low-rank principal gradient subspace from sample-wise gradients of the current task. The server merges historical protection directions from different clients, and subsequent local training projects gradients onto the orthogonal complement of the protected subspace to reduce interference between new-task updates and knowledge accumulated across clients. FedSubMerge-AD further selects related clients according to layer-wise subspace distances and constructs client-specific protection spaces, thereby regulating the balance between historical knowledge coverage and plasticity for new tasks. Experiments across multiple medical imaging modalities, data-heterogeneity settings, client scales, and participation rates show that the proposed methods improve overall federated performance and knowledge retention without replaying historical raw samples, while confining additional subspace exchange to task boundaries.

(3) To address limited supervision coverage and historical forgetting when sparse scribble annotations are combined with evolving tasks, this thesis develops ScribbleCL and proposes ZSDERpp for different continual segmentation scenarios. For the current task, the method augments partial cross-entropy with global consistency and a spatial prior to constrain unlabeled regions. For historical tasks, a capacity-limited buffer stores a small number of image--scribble pairs, reference features, and task information, and subsequent updates jointly use feature preservation, buffer partial cross-entropy, global consistency, and the spatial prior. For class-incremental segmentation, background-probability regrouping and consistency with the previous model are introduced to reduce the risk that old classes are incorrectly absorbed into the changing background semantics. Experiments in a cross-center domain-incremental setting show that the weak-supervision constraints improve learning from sparse annotations and that limited historical replay further enhances final multi-domain segmentation and retention of previous domains. The results also indicate that historical retention, current-task modeling, and forward generalization to future domains should be evaluated separately.

(4) To mitigate forgetting and insufficient forward generalization when anatomical regions, imaging modalities, and registration relationships change sequentially, this thesis proposes SAMCL, a sharpness-aware meta-continual learning method for medical image registration. The method performs experience replay using a capacity-limited set of historical image pairs, coordinates update directions from current and historical tasks through first-order meta-continual learning, and incorporates sharpness-aware optimization into both update paths. Finite replay therefore provides evidence from previous tasks, the meta-update reduces gradient interference across tasks, and the sharpness-aware objective improves local robustness around the learned solution. Sequential experiments involving different organs, modalities, and registration types show that SAMCL alleviates the degradation of historical-task performance caused by direct sequential training and maintains balanced performance across evaluations of current-task learning, historical-task retention, and forward generalization.

Overall, this thesis studies continual learning for medical image analysis at the levels of problem formulation, evaluation methodology, and learning algorithms. The results show that the effectiveness of a continual learning method depends not only on whether it reduces forgetting, but also on how tasks evolve, whether current supervision is complete, how historical information is retained, and how multi-center data can participate in collaborative training. Reliable evaluation therefore requires explicit access conditions and a joint analysis of stability, plasticity, forward generalization, and resource cost. The studies in this thesis provide methodological and experimental foundations for building medical image analysis models that can be updated continually under different forms of limited training information.
